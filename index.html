Compared with the U.S. national report (NCES)

The National Center for Education Statistics published Highlights of U.S. PISA 2025 Results (NCES 2026-032). We checked its main findings against the analysis on this page. The numbers and the conclusions are the same. One headline sentence goes further than the data, though NCES's own detail does not. And NCES is more careful than a simple reading of the data about results by background.

Read all U.S. results with caution

The U.S. sample did not meet PISA's standards: 45% of sampled schools took part before replacement (54% after), and 76% of sampled students. The OECD's Adjudication Group concluded that "it is not possible to exclude the possibility of bias on performance indicators, nor to determine its most likely direction" (PISA 2025 Technical Report, Chapter 18). Under adverse but hypothetical scenarios, the distortion could reach about 15% of a standard deviation.

Where both agree the data cannot answer

Not reportedResults by family background and by race or ethnicity

More than 40% of U.S. students did not receive the student questionnaire: about half because 9 of the 50 states chose not to administer it, and about half because their schools tested early in the testing window. The OECD recommended "limited reporting of questionnaire results", and NCES did not report results by socio-economic status or by race or ethnicity. On this page, U.S. results that rely on the student questionnaire (family background, immigrant background and students' answers) carry a warning: they leave out those 9 states entirely, and no statistical adjustment can bring them back.

Where the results are the same

Mean scores (reading 490, mathematics 463, science 502) and the change since 2022 (reading down 14 points, significant; mathematics and science not significantly different), the number of education systems scoring higher, similar and lower, the shares of low and top performers, the 90th-to-10th percentile gap in reading (312 points), and the gender gap: in mathematics it widened from 13 to 24 points in favour of boys, a significant change, while in reading and science it did not change significantly. NCES compares with 89 other education systems, including Cyprus, which is not in the public file.

Latvia: what changed, and for whom

Latvia's national presentation of the results (University of Latvia, 8 September 2026) was not accessible to us, so this section compares with the OECD's country note for Latvia and adds an analysis of the public data.

The language of the test changed

Between 2023 and 2025 Latvia completed a transition to Latvian as the sole language of instruction in compulsory education, and "starting with 2023, all centralized tests (including PISA) were held in Latvian for all students". "In 2022, some 28% of students had taken the PISA test in Russian" (OECD country note). Changes since 2022 therefore mix two things: what students learned, and the language in which they were tested.

Read with the test language in mindWhere the decline is concentrated

Change since 2022 for students who speak Latvian at home: reading −20 points (±7), mathematics −12 (±6), science −3 (±8, not significant). For students who speak another language at home, mostly Russian: reading −95 (±10), mathematics −43 (±9), science −69 (±11). In 2022 most of these students were tested in Russian, in 2025 in Latvian. Part of their drop may reflect taking the test in a second language rather than a loss of knowledge, and the data cannot separate the two. "Latvian at home" means tested in Latvian and speaking the test language at home, a definition that is comparable across the two cycles. An independent analysis of the same public data (J. Ošlejs, nra.lv, September 2026) reports the same 2025 results for students who speak Latvian at home.

Where the results are the same

Mean scores fell in all three subjects and were "the lowest ever observed in PISA in Latvia", and the shares of low and top performers match the OECD's tables. The OECD states that "in Latvia, all data met the quality standards set by PISA".

RAMA's report on PISA 2025 (September 2026) is Israel's official publication. We checked its main findings against the analysis on this page. Most numbers are the same. In a few places, this analysis leads to a different conclusion.

RAMA shows score gaps between students by factors such as AI use, family support, phone distraction and teacher shortage, with a caution that they are not causal. This page does not show such gaps, because they mostly reflect other differences between students (see the class-size example).

Where the results are the same

Means and changes (reading 436, down 38; mathematics 433, down 25; science 442, down 23), shares of low and top performers, the trends for Hebrew and Arabic speakers (down 44 and 21 points in reading), low performers by language, and the principals' and students' reports (teacher shortage 50% against 39% in the OECD, "school has been a waste of time" 37% against 24%, phone ban 30% against 49%). As in RAMA's report, Haredi education is shown for girls only, and their scores match the report (reading 503, mathematics 455, science 466).

Only in RAMA's report

Trends by sector between 2022 and 2025 (for example, a 52-point drop in reading in state education) and by sector and gender. The public 2022 file does not identify sector, so they cannot be computed from open data.

Whether an effect differs between groups

Understated by the data

Questions such as "does family background matter more in some schools than in others?" ask how an effect differs between groups. The model that produces PISA scores does not include such differences, so the released data show them smaller than they are.

What students and principals report

How common, for whom, and change

PISA asks students and principals about school life. These answers are facts worth reporting in their own right. This section shows how common each answer is. It does not link answers to scores.

Why did scores change?

PISA cannot answer this

PISA tests each student once and does not assign schools or policies. It shows what goes together, not what causes what. A gap in scores between students who differ on one question usually reflects many other differences between them.

A worked example from Israel: class size

In Israel, students in larger classes score higher in mathematics. This is not because crowding helps. Larger classes are more common in schools with more advantaged students. Taking only school background and sector into account cuts the gap by about 40%, and other differences remain unmeasured.

What PISA can do for a "why" claim is check its premise. If a claim says a decline is due to a teacher shortage, the section above shows whether principals reported more shortage in 2025 than in 2022. A factor that did not change cannot by itself explain a change.

Methods: estimates use all ten plausible values and the 80 replicate weights (Fay's method). Changes in mean scores and percentiles add the OECD's linking error (PISA 2025 Technical Report, Annex Table 15.A.20); changes in shares do not, so their margins are slightly too narrow. Proficiency levels use the cut-offs of PISA 2022 reports. Differences between countries are tested as independent samples. Coverage of 15-year-olds is Coverage Index 3 (Annex Table 14.A.1). Groups: family background thirds are cut within each country and cycle. Groups from the sample design use each country's own sampling strata and labels; for Israel they are mapped to sectors. Principals' answers are shown as the share of students in their schools. Questionnaire items keep the same ID and wording in 2022 and 2025. Checked against the OECD's published tables (PISA 2025 Results, Volume I, Annex B1): means, standard errors, percentiles, shares of low and top performers and changes since 2022 match for every country.
